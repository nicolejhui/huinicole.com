n 2019 I was diagnosed with Type 1 Diabetes. So during my final months of high school, I found myself attending diabetes education classes to learn how to manage this early graduation gift from the universe. 
My care team did an amazing job with my transition. They got me on a Dexcom provided plenty of diabetes education: carb counting, insulin ratios, meal timings, glycemic indexes, bolus vs basal – the whole shebang. They had even given me a small booklet to carry around with carbohydrate counts of common foods and meals from major restaurant chains.
By the time graduation rolled around, I could bolus (take fast-acting insulin) for a classic chicken sandwich like a pro, but I struggled to keep my blood sugar in range for many dishes I had grown up eating as an Asian American. 
Any google search for a specific cultural dish or food yielded vague results, and there was little discussion about glycemic indexes for such dishes either. In fact, forums centered on type 1 diabetes and diabetes often demonized Asian food and complained about the unpredictability of them. My first time eating dim-sum, a beloved Hong Kong tradition, was on Christmas morning; while I knew the dishes were high-carb, I neglected to account for the high glycemic index and ended up at dinner with a blood sugar of 400. 
Even with home-cooking, I still sometimes struggle with getting an accurate carbohydrate count and insulin dosage. For example, while congee doesn’t use much rice, it spikes your blood sugar rapidly due to the cooking methodology. The past 7 years as a type 1 diabetic, I’ve had to learn through trial and error how to navigate insulin dosages for dishes where blood sugar impact isn’t as widely discussed, often relying on a standard 1:8 insulin-carb ratio and going by “vibes.” 
Today, there has been a rise in apps that focus on macro counting based on image recognition thanks to the wealth of nutritional data that exists today. However, much of this data is based on western cuisine, and even fewer focus on the actual glucose impact. East Asians have the lowest incident rates for type 1 diabetes, with over 90% of diabetes cases being type 2 instead, meaning there aren’t many resources for me to figure out how to dose correctly for a standard Asian diet. 
That’s why I’m building a tool of my own: SikFan, which means “To eat a meal” in Cantonese, to help me better bolus for foods I commonly eat by predicting the impact of on MY blood glucose trained on a collection of meal data and blood glucose patterns.
